What's happening

    How they fit together
    • They all share one rhythm. Three steps and a tap, twice, except the triple, which fills the tap with two quick steps instead.
    • Side basic is the default. Most of a song lives there. In place holds the same spot on a crowded floor.
    • Box and diagonal patterns are the Dominican root. Same count, but the travel is a square instead of a line.
    • The triple is a rhythm swap, not a new step. Once it is solid, it drops into any of the others wherever a tap falls.
    • Grapevine travels. Use it when there is floor space to cover.
    • The diagonal box is the box turned 45 degrees. Same count, but it opens the floor up in two directions at once.
    • Double tap and the triple are both rhythm swaps, not new steps. Learn them once and drop them into any pattern wherever a tap falls.
    • The pivot turn is the basic with half a turn between the first three steps. The facing arrow shows which way the body points.
    Before stepping
    • Soft knees. Stay slightly down the whole time. Never lock them straight, this is what makes it look like dancing instead of walking.
    • Chest up, shoulders back. Tall spine. The lead comes from the chest, so this is the steering.
    • Weight fully on one foot. Commit. Half-and-half is what a partner feels as hesitant.
    • Eyes up. Not down at the floor, since looking down collapses everything above it.
    The three beginner mistakes
    • Locked knees. The hip movement comes from bending and straightening the knees. Straight legs, no hips, it's that simple.
    • Forcing the hips. Don't push them. Let them happen from the weight shift and the knees.
    • Watching the floor. Learn it here, then look up. On the floor the eyes belong up.

    What is not here. Some footwork does not survive a top-down diagram. Ochos, boleos and free Dominican footwork depend on body rotation, leg lifts and slides that a two-foot overhead view cannot show honestly. For those, search "bachata ochos tutorial", "bachata boleo" or "Dominican bachata free footwork" and watch a real dancer.

    A note on names and variants. Bachata footwork is a living folk tradition, schools disagree on names and on which foot starts. This follows the convention that the leader's basic travels left first (starting left foot) and the follower mirrors it. If a school teaches the opposite, just use the Follower toggle. Pattern descriptions cross-checked against iASO Records' Dominican bachata guide and the Library of Dance bachata syllabus.

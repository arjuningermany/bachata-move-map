What you are hearing
  • The güira is the metal scraper running underneath everything. It is the steadiest thing in the mix, which is why dancers use it to hold the tempo rather than the melody.
  • The bongo marks the accents. The open hit that lands on 4 and 8 is the same place the tap and hip movement go, so the music is telling the body where the accent belongs.
  • The bass lands on the downbeats and is the loudest clue to where the 1 sits.
  • The 1 is marked strongest here on purpose. In a real song it is subtler, so treat this as training wheels and then go and test yourself on actual music.
Why phrases matter more than counts
  • Music comes in phrases, usually four eight-counts long. The bar above shows where you are in one, with the first block gold.
  • Start moves at the start of a phrase and they feel intentional. Start them halfway through and the same move feels like it arrived by accident.
  • Resolve at the end of a phrase. Land back in open position as the phrase closes and a partner feels the dance breathe.
  • Leave the last eight alone sometimes. A held pause on a phrase ending reads as far more musical than another turn.
How to practise this
  • Listen first, do nothing. Two or three phrases with no movement, just locating the 1.
  • Then hide the counts. Turn off "show the counts" and find the 1 by ear alone. This is the actual skill.
  • Then start mid-phrase. With that on, the groove begins at a random point, so you cannot count from the top and have to hear it.
  • Then go to real music. Put on any bachata track and tap the 1 with no help at all. That is the transfer test.

Bachata sits around 120 to 130 BPM. Slower is easier to hear; faster is closer to a social floor.
